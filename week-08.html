In-class graduate synthesis presentation

To be assigned · About the presentations

Unlike other science fields such as maths and physics, ecology and evolution hold few laws. One of them may be the species-area rule, in which species richness increases with area. However, this is not true of all regions. Some islands of the same size can have different numbers of species; why? MacArthur and Wilson (1963) reduced the answer to a balance between colonization and extinction, predicting that species number depends on island area and isolation rather than on which species are present. Islands represent a filtered, not random, sample of mainland diversity: good dispersers arrive while poor ones rarely do, producing disharmonious faunas (missing whole groups common on the mainland) and the nested subsets that biogeographers have long recognized. Once established, colonists often evolve in predictable ways (the island syndrome of giants, dwarfs, and tameness), and on large enough islands speciation begins to add species from within (an “evolutionary” species-area concept). Today, as humans carry species from island to island, does the equilibrium MacArthur and Wilson envisioned still hold?
