In-class graduate synthesis presentation

To be assigned · About the presentations

Alfred Russel Wallace divided the world into zoogeographical regions, recognizing that where species live carries the signature of geological history. His line through the Malay Archipelago, separating Asian from Australasian faunas across a strait only a few dozen kilometers wide, remains one of biogeography’s starkest boundaries, a divide later explained by plate tectonics and by deep water that stayed open even when Ice Age sea levels fell. At the global scale, the latitudinal diversity gradient (more species toward the tropics) is one of the most general patterns in ecology, yet ecologists have weighed competing explanations for much of the past century. In the late 1960’s, Dan Janzen argued that because tropical organisms experience little seasonal change in temperature, they should be physiologically constrained to tropical environments and so unable to disperse out into temperate regions. In doing so, Janzen presented an important new hypothesis that may explain the latitudinal diversity gradient, linking thermal physiology to dispersal limitation and, potentially, to endemism. These ideas continue to be important today as we consider the role of contemporary climate change on the redistribution of global biodiversity.
